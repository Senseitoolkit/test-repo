Creating Thinking Students

The essentials, and only the essentials: the phrases to use, the routines to run, the reflection points to fix in every lesson, and the habits that make students do the thinking instead of waiting for you to do it for them.

Think · the five essentials

If you only change five things, change these

Everything else in this guide is a way of doing one of these five things. Each comes with a Try this week you can start tomorrow.

1
Wait, then wait againThree to five seconds, both directions

Leave three to five seconds of silence after you ask a question, and the same after a student answers. Most of the thinking in a classroom happens in the pause, and most teachers fill it inside one second.

Try this week

Count to five in your head before you call on anyone. After an answer, say nothing and look expectant. Students will add more.

2
Ask for the reasoning, not the answerAfter right answers as often as wrong ones

An answer ends thinking; a reason starts it. Ask "What makes you say that?" after every answer, right or wrong, so that giving a reason becomes normal rather than a sign you've made a mistake.

Try this week

For one week, never say "correct" or "no" straight away. Ask for the reasoning first, then let the class judge.

3
Use a few routines, oftenFour to six, until students can lead them

A familiar structure frees working memory for the content, and students start reaching for the routine themselves. Depth comes from repetition, not variety.

Try this week

One routine per week for the first month. Display the steps. Don't introduce a new one until the last one runs without you.

4
Fix the reflection pointsStart, hinge and close, every lesson

Reflection happens when it has a slot. Build three fixed points into every lesson and reflection stops being the thing that gets dropped when time runs short.

Try this week

Put the closing reflection in your plan before the main activity, and give it a fixed time. Protect it.

5
Make not-knowing safe"I don't know yet" is a legitimate first answer

Students will not think out loud where it is risky. Mistakes are information, and changing your mind is something to be proud of.

Try this week

Share your own changed mind in front of the class: "I used to think... but now I think..." Then celebrate the first student who does the same.

✓
The testOne question at the end of any lesson

Who did the thinking? If you explained, demonstrated, answered and summarised, the thinking was yours. The goal is a lesson where students notice, reason, argue, revise and sum up, and you mostly ask.

Why this works. Thinking is a habit before it is a skill. Students learn what a classroom values from what happens hundreds of times a day, not from what is on the wall. Wait time, "what makes you say that?", a repeated routine and a fixed reflection slot are small, but they happen every lesson, and that is the point.
Challenge · the phrase bank

What you say shapes what students do with their minds

Filter by what you need the phrase to do, then pick two or three to use until they're automatic. Tap copy to grab a set for your planner.

What do you need the phrase to do?

What do you notice?

Open up

Low-risk entry point. There is no wrong noticing, so everyone can start, and the noticings become the raw material for the real question.

What might be going on here?

Open up

"Might" signals that you want possibilities, not the answer. It invites tentative thinking from students who would stay silent for "What is happening?"

What are some possibilities?

Open up

"Some" asks for more than one. It stops the first answer from closing the discussion.

What's your first thought? It doesn't have to be right.

Open up

Lowers the stakes explicitly. Useful for a class that has learned to wait for the clever student.

What makes you say that?

Press

The single most useful phrase in this guide. Asks for reasoning without judging the answer. Use it after right answers as often as wrong ones.

How do you know?

Press

Shifts from opinion to evidence. Works in maths, reading, science and the playground.

Can you walk me through your thinking?

Press

Asks for the process, not the product. Reveals where understanding is solid and where it is a guess.

Convince me.

Press

Playful but demanding. Students must build an argument rather than wait for you to confirm.

Say more about that.

Extend

Keeps the student talking past their first, usually thin, answer. Pair with silence.

What else?

Extend

Two words. Says "that was good and I think there is more". Keep asking until the ideas run dry.

Is that always true?

Extend

Pushes toward generalisation and counter-examples. The engine of mathematical and scientific thinking.

What would happen if...?

Extend

Turns a fact into a model students can run. Change one variable and ask them to predict.

Who can add to what Yuki said?

Connect

Makes ideas belong to the class, not to you. Students listen to each other when they know they'll be asked to build on it.

Do you agree or disagree with Ken? Why?

Connect

Forces a position and a reason. "Why" is not optional; without it this is a vote.

Can you say what Mia said in your own words?

Connect

Checks listening and understanding at once, and gives the original speaker the experience of being heard.

Who thought something different?

Connect

Protects disagreement. Without it, the first confident answer becomes the class answer.

What strategy did you use?

Metacognition

Names the thinking so it can be reused. Students who can name a strategy can choose it next time.

Where did you get stuck, and what did you do?

Metacognition

Treats being stuck as a normal, discussable event with moves attached, rather than a failure.

How confident are you, and why?

Metacognition

Separates knowing from guessing. A student who says "about half sure, because I didn't check" has told you exactly what to teach.

What would you do differently next time?

Metacognition

The question that turns an experience into learning. Ask it after successes too.

What did you used to think? What do you think now?

Reflect

The clearest way to make learning visible to the learner. Works at the end of a lesson, a week or a unit.

What's one thing that changed in your thinking today?

Reflect

"One thing" is achievable for every student. "Changed" signals that changing your mind is the goal.

What's still puzzling you?

Reflect

Legitimises unfinished understanding and gives you tomorrow's starting point.

What would you tell someone who missed today?

Reflect

A summary in the student's words, framed as helping rather than being tested.

What do you know for sure?

Stuck

Finds solid ground. Most stuck students know more than they think; they've lost the thread, not the knowledge.

What's one small thing you could try?

Stuck

Shrinks the task to a single move. The move doesn't need to work; it needs to restart thinking.

What would you tell a friend who was stuck here?

Stuck

Students can often coach a friend through a problem they "can't" do themselves. Borrow that.

What question would help you right now?

Stuck

Hands the next step back to the student. Asking for the right question is itself a thinking skill.

Routine · the core eight

Eight routines that cover the whole inquiry cycle

Open any routine for the steps, what to say, what to watch for, and how it changes from Reception to Year 6. You don't need all eight; four used weekly beat eight used once.

Stage of inquiry

See – Think – Wonder

observe, interpret, question

Slow looking at an image, object, text or data before anyone is asked to explain it. The best way into a new topic.

IntroducingExploring10–15 minWhole class or pairs
▸
Steps
  1. See. Collect observations; push for detail with "What else?" "What do you see? Only what is actually there, no guessing yet."
  2. Think. Every interpretation must point back to something seen. "What do you think is going on? What makes you say that?"
  3. Wonder. Record every wonder; these become the unit's questions. "What does this make you wonder?"
  4. Keep the wonders visible. Revisit them at the midpoint and end of the unit and tick off the ones answered.
Watch for
  • Students jumping to "think" during "see". Gently send them back: "Is that something you see, or something you think?"
  • You answering the wonders. Don't. Park them.
What it looks like, Reception to Year 6
Reception – Year 1

Oral, one step at a time, with a single striking image. Use "I see... I think... I wonder..." as a chant. Draw the wonders.

Year 2 – Year 3

Three-column chart, pairs talk before sharing. Sentence stems on the board.

Year 4 – Year 6

Written individually first, then shared. Sort wonders into "we can find out" and "we can only argue about". Use with primary sources, graphs and poems.

Think – Pair – Share (done properly)

think alone, rehearse, report

The most used and most misused routine. Done well, every student thinks, every student speaks, and sharing reports the pair's thinking, not one person's.

Any stage3–5 minPairs
▸
Steps
  1. Think, silently. This is the step that gets skipped, and the one that matters. "Thirty seconds on your own. No talking, no hands."
  2. Pair. Give a sentence starter if needed. "Partner A goes first. Partner B, your job is to listen and then say what you heard."
  3. Share. Reporting the partner's idea makes listening matter. "Tell me what your partner thinks."
  4. Cold call for the share. Everyone has something because everyone has talked.
Watch for
  • Pairs where one talks and one nods. Rotate who goes first, and ask the listener to report.
  • Asking "Who wants to share?" This undoes the point. Call on anyone.
What it looks like, Reception to Year 6
Reception – Year 1

"Knee to knee, eye to eye". One short question. Model a pair conversation with a puppet or a colleague first.

Year 2 – Year 3

Add a timer the children can see. Teach "Can you say more?" as the listener's job.

Year 4 – Year 6

Think step becomes a quick written jot. Share step can be "Did your partner change your mind at all?"

What Makes You Say That?

claim, reason, compare

A routine small enough to be a reflex. Every claim is followed by a request for the reasoning behind it.

Deepening1 min, any timeWhole class
▸
Steps
  1. A student makes a claim, gives an answer or shares an opinion.
  2. Keep your face neutral. Do not confirm or deny the claim first. "What makes you say that?"
  3. Accept the reasoning, then open it up. "Does anyone see it differently? What makes you say that?"
  4. Only now, if needed, add your own view, as one more voice with reasons.
Watch for
  • Using it only on wrong answers. Students will learn it means "you're wrong" and stop answering. Use it most on right answers.
What it looks like, Reception to Year 6
Reception – Year 1

Rephrase as "How do you know?" or "Show me". Accept pointing, drawing and acting out as reasons.

Year 2 – Year 3

Teach the stem "I think... because..." Make a poster. Praise the "because", not the answer.

Year 4 – Year 6

Students ask it of each other in group work. Distinguish types of reasons: evidence, experience, logic, authority.

I Used to Think... Now I Think...

recall, compare, explain the change

The reflection routine that shows students their own learning. Essential at the end of a unit; useful at the end of a lesson.

Reflecting5–10 minIndividual then pairs
▸
Steps
  1. Remind students what they thought at the start. Show their original See-Think-Wonder, 3-2-1 Bridge or first draft if you have it.
  2. Write or say it. "Finish this sentence: I used to think..."
  3. "Now finish this one: Now I think..."
  4. Pairs share. "What changed your mind? Was it something you read, saw, did or heard someone say?"
  5. Collect a few publicly and celebrate the biggest shifts.
Watch for
  • "I used to think nothing, now I know everything." Push for a specific before and after: "What exactly did you think a shadow was?"
What it looks like, Reception to Year 6
Reception – Year 1

Oral, with you scribing. Pair with a "before" drawing and an "after" drawing of the same thing.

Year 2 – Year 3

Two-box template. Add a third box: "What changed my mind was..."

Year 4 – Year 6

Written reflection in the learning journal. Ask also: "What do I still think, and why hasn't that changed?"

Connect – Extend – Challenge

link, stretch, question

For processing new input: a text, a video, a visitor, an experiment. Links the new to the known and surfaces what doesn't fit.

DeepeningReflecting10 minIndividual then groups
▸
Steps
  1. Connect. "How does this connect to what you already knew?"
  2. Extend. "What new ideas pushed your thinking further or in a new direction?"
  3. Challenge. "What is still confusing, or what do you want to argue with?"
  4. Collect the challenges. They are the best questions for the next lesson.
Watch for
  • Empty "challenge" boxes. Model one yourself: "What challenged me was that the author never says why..."
What it looks like, Reception to Year 6
Reception – Year 1

Two steps only: "That reminds me of..." and "I didn't know that..." Oral, in a circle.

Year 2 – Year 3

Three sticky notes in three colours, posted on a class chart.

Year 4 – Year 6

Written after reading, used as the basis for a discussion. Students respond to each other's challenges.

Claim – Support – Question

assert, evidence, test

The reasoning routine for maths, science and reading: say what you think is true, back it up, then ask what would test it.

Deepening15 minPairs or groups
▸
Steps
  1. Claim. "What do you think is true here? Say it in one sentence."
  2. Support. "What have you seen, read, measured or worked out that supports it?"
  3. Question. "What's still uncertain? What would make you change your claim?"
  4. Pairs swap and try to find a hole in each other's support.
Watch for
  • Support that is just the claim repeated louder. Ask "Where could I see that for myself?"
What it looks like, Reception to Year 6
Reception – Year 1

"I think... because I saw..." with a real object or experiment in front of them. Skip the question step.

Year 2 – Year 3

Three-row table. In maths: "I think the answer is... because... but I'm not sure about..."

Year 4 – Year 6

Students evaluate the strength of each other's support. Introduce "That's an example, not a proof."

Chalk Talk

write, respond, connect

A silent written conversation on large paper. Every student contributes, quiet students lead, and ideas build in a way talk rarely allows.

Exploring15 minWhole class, moving
▸
Steps
  1. Write a question or provocation in the centre of each of four to six large sheets.
  2. "No talking. Write a response, or draw one. Then move to another sheet."
  3. Second pass. "Now respond to what someone else wrote. Draw a line to connect ideas. Add a question."
  4. End with a gallery walk and a spoken debrief. "What surprised you on the sheets?"
Watch for
  • Breaking the silence yourself. Point, don't speak.
  • Sheets with one idea written five times. Prompt "Respond, don't repeat."
What it looks like, Reception to Year 6
Reception – Year 1

One sheet, drawings and single words, you scribe what children tell you quietly. Five minutes.

Year 2 – Year 3

Three sheets with picture prompts. Teach the "connecting line" and the "question mark" moves.

Year 4 – Year 6

Use for contested questions and unit reflections. Require every student to add one connection and one question.

3 – 2 – 1 Bridge

snapshot, repeat, bridge

A before-and-after snapshot. Done at the start of a unit and again at the end, the gap between the two is the learning.

IntroducingReflecting5 min each timeIndividual
▸
Steps
  1. At the start. "Three words that come to mind about this topic. Two questions. One picture or comparison: this topic is like..."
  2. Keep it. Don't discuss it much.
  3. At the end. Repeat all three, on a new sheet.
  4. Bridge. Put the two side by side. "How did your thinking change? Which of your questions got answered?"
Watch for
  • Forgetting to do the second half. Put it in the plan now.
What it looks like, Reception to Year 6
Reception – Year 1

One word, one question, one drawing. You scribe the word and question.

Year 2 – Year 3

Simple three-row template, kept in the learning journal.

Year 4 – Year 6

Full version. The "bridge" reflection becomes a short paragraph and a self-assessment of a thinking skill.

Support · reflection points

Reflection is not a mood; it is a slot in the plan

Three fixed points in every lesson, three in every unit, and one for you. Each point needs two minutes and one question.

Think · classroom habits

The habits that make it stick

Phrases and routines only work inside a culture where thinking is expected of everyone, every time. Introduce these explicitly and name them when you use them.

Wait time, both directions

Three to five seconds after your question. Three to five seconds after their answer. Teach the class that silence means thinking is happening, not that something has gone wrong.

"I'm going to give you proper thinking time. Don't put your hand up; just think."

No hands up (except to ask)

Hands up lets half the class opt out. Think time, then call on anyone, warmly. Hands are for asking a question, not answering one.

"Hands down. Everyone's thinking about this. I'll come to a few of you in a moment."

No opting out

"I don't know" is a legitimate start, not an end. Give a scaffold, or take an answer from someone else and come back, so the first student finishes by saying something.

"You don't know yet. What's your best guess? ... Ken, can you help? ... Now, Mia, say it in your own words."

Talk partners that rotate

Every student has a partner, partners change every two weeks, and the partner's job is defined: listen, then say it back. Pair talk is where most of the thinking gets rehearsed.

"Partner B, your only job is to listen so well you could say it for them."

Build on, don't repeat

Teach the listening moves: track the speaker, add to what was said, disagree with a reason, say it back. Discussion is a chain, not a list.

"Before you share yours, tell us how it connects to what Yuki said."

A thinking vocabulary

Students can only do thinking they have words for. Teach, display and use: notice, wonder, predict, evidence, reason, compare, perspective, revise, convince.

"That's a prediction. What's your evidence for it?"

Celebrate changed minds

Make changing your mind the most praised thing in the room. Model it yourself. Students praised for being right will defend wrong answers; students praised for revising will think.

"Ken just changed his mind because of Mia's evidence. That is exactly what good thinkers do."

Mistakes are data

Put a wrong answer on the board and work on it together. Ask what the thinking behind it might have been. Nobody is named; everybody learns.

"Here's an answer someone gave. It's wrong, but it's a sensible kind of wrong. What was the thinking?"

Challenge · the first four weeks

Add one thing a week

Don't try to do all of this at once. Keep the previous weeks going, and by the end of the month the classroom will feel different.

1Two phrases and a pause
  • Count to five after every question
  • "What makes you say that?" after every answer
  • One closing reflection, every lesson: one thing learned, one thing unsure
2One routine, done properly
  • Introduce Think-Pair-Share with the silent think step and the "report your partner" share
  • Hands down becomes the default
  • Run See-Think-Wonder once, keep the wonders up
3The hinge and the swap
  • Add a mid-lesson hinge check with mini whiteboards
  • Pick three swaps from the phrase bank and tape them to your desk
  • Friday: "I used to think / now I think" on the week
4Hand it over
  • A student leads one routine
  • Students use "What makes you say that?" on each other in group work
  • Do your sixty-second teacher reflection after every lesson
After the first month, add one new routine every two or three weeks until you have four to six you use constantly. Then stop adding. Depth comes from repetition, not variety: the moment a Year 3 child says "Can we do See-Think-Wonder on this?" you have a thinking classroom.
