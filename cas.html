CAS Guide

Creativity, Activity, Service is the part of the diploma that earns no points and still decides whether the diploma is awarded. This guide covers what counts, how to show the seven learning outcomes, how to run the CAS project, and how to write reflections that a coordinator can sign off.

Duration

At least 18 months

Regular involvement from the start of year 1, ideally every week.

Evidence

7 learning outcomes

Each one shown at least once, with evidence in the portfolio.

Project

1 CAS project

Collaborative, planned by students, lasting a month or more.

Meetings

3 interviews

Documented conversations with the CAS coordinator or adviser.

The school decides whether CAS is complete

The IB sets the requirements above. Your school sets the deadlines, the platform for the portfolio and any extra expectations, and the CAS coordinator confirms completion to the IB. Where this guide and your coordinator differ, follow your coordinator.

The Three Strands

Aim for a reasonable balance across all three over the 18 months. One experience can cover more than one strand.

Creativity

Exploring and extending ideas to make an original or interpretive product or performance.

Good fits

  • Learning an instrument, joining a band, choir or orchestra
  • Theatre, dance, film-making, photography, design
  • Writing, coding a game or app, building a website
  • Debate, Model United Nations, a student magazine

Make it stronger

  • Set a goal with an end product: a performance, an exhibition, a published piece
  • Choose something that stretches you past what you already do well

Activity

Physical exertion that contributes to a healthy lifestyle.

Good fits

  • Team and individual sports, in school or in a club
  • Running, swimming, cycling, climbing, hiking
  • Martial arts, yoga, dance training
  • A personal fitness plan with measured targets

Make it stronger

  • Record a starting point and a target: a distance, a time, a grading, a skill
  • If you already play a sport, add a new role such as coaching juniors or learning to referee

Service

Working with a community, unpaid, in response to a real need that the community recognises.

Four kinds of service

  • Direct: working with people, animals or the environment face to face, such as tutoring
  • Indirect: helping without meeting those who benefit, such as redesigning a charity's website
  • Advocacy: speaking up for a cause, such as an awareness campaign
  • Research: gathering and reporting information to inform action, such as a waste audit

Make it stronger

  • Ask the community what it needs before you plan anything
  • Prefer a long commitment to one group over many one-off events

What Counts as a CAS Experience

An experience can be a single event or a long series. Test each idea against these four questions before you start.

Four tests

  1. Does it fit a strand? It belongs to creativity, activity, service, or a mix.
  2. Is it yours? It comes from a personal interest, a skill, a talent or a chance to grow.
  3. Will you develop? It gives room to show learner profile attributes such as being a risk-taker, caring or reflective.
  4. Is it separate from your courses? It is not used for, or part of, any assessed diploma work.

Usually counts

  • A new challenge with a goal you set yourself
  • A continuing activity where you take on a new role or target
  • Voluntary work arranged with a real organisation or group
  • A school club, when you take an active part and can show growth

Usually does not count

  • Anything you are paid for
  • Work that is part of a diploma subject, the Extended Essay or TOK
  • Ordinary family duties and household chores
  • Passive attendance: watching a match, visiting a museum, sitting in an audience
  • Simple, repetitive tasks with no learning, such as filing or stacking shelves
  • Religious devotion or political campaigning that could divide groups. Ask your coordinator about service linked to a faith community
  • Fundraising with no understanding of where the money goes or why it is needed
  • Anything started and finished before the diploma began

Borderline case? Ask the coordinator before you begin. Approval in advance is much easier than arguing afterwards.

About hours

The IB does not ask for a set number of hours. It asks for regular involvement over at least 18 months and evidence of the seven outcomes. Some schools still set their own hour guides or a minimum number of experiences, so check what yours expects. Counting hours is useful for seeing balance across the strands, and no substitute for reflection.

The Seven Learning Outcomes

Completion rests on these. Each must be shown at least once across the whole programme. No single experience needs all seven.

1

Know your strengths and where you need to grow

You can describe what you are good at and what you find hard, and you choose experiences with that in mind.

Evidence: a starting self-assessment, a skills audit, before-and-after comparisons.

Ask yourself: what did this show me about myself that I did not know in September?

2

Take on challenges and build new skills

You try something unfamiliar, or push an existing activity further, and can name the skills gained.

Evidence: first attempts next to later ones, a certificate, a recording, a coach's comment.

Ask yourself: what can I do now that I could not do at the start, and what made it hard?

3

Initiate and plan an experience

You take an idea from a first thought to a workable plan, alone or with others.

Evidence: a proposal, a timeline, a budget, emails to organisers, meeting notes.

Ask yourself: what did I decide, and what would I plan differently next time?

4

Show commitment and perseverance

You keep going regularly over time, including when it becomes dull or difficult.

Evidence: an attendance record, dated entries across months, a supervisor's statement.

Ask yourself: when did I want to stop, and what kept me going?

5

Work with others and see the benefit

You can explain what collaboration added, what was difficult about it, and your own part in the team.

Evidence: team roles, shared planning documents, feedback from teammates.

Ask yourself: what did the group achieve that I could not have done alone?

6

Engage with issues of global significance

You connect what you do locally to a wider issue, such as poverty, health, the environment or inequality.

Evidence: background research, a link to a UN Sustainable Development Goal, action taken as a result.

Ask yourself: how does this local action relate to a problem people face elsewhere?

7

Consider the ethics of choices and actions

You think about the consequences of what you do and decide with them in mind.

Evidence: a reflection on a dilemma you met: consent for photos, who really benefits, cost to the environment.

Ask yourself: who was affected by my choices, and was the outcome fair to them?

Outcomes students most often leave until too late

  • Outcome 3, because joining existing clubs involves no planning of your own. The CAS project usually covers it.
  • Outcome 6, because sport and arts activities rarely touch global issues unless you make the link on purpose.
  • Outcome 7, because ethical questions go unnoticed unless you look for them. Service work nearly always raises one.

The CAS Stages

The five-step frame for any longer experience and for the project. Reflection can happen at any point, not only at the end.

1

Investigation

List your interests and skills. For service, find out what the community needs by asking the people involved.

2

Preparation

Set roles, a timeline and resources. Learn any skills you need. Get approval and check safety.

3

Action

Carry out the plan. Solve problems as they come. Collect evidence as you go.

4

Reflection

Describe what happened, how you felt, what you learned and what you would change.

5

Demonstration

Share what you did and learned: the portfolio, a presentation, an assembly, a display.

The CAS Project

Every student completes at least one. It is the best single source of evidence in the portfolio.

The rules

  • It is collaborative. You work with other students or members of the wider community.
  • It lasts at least one month from planning to completion.
  • Students initiate and plan it. A teacher-run trip you only attend is not a project.
  • It covers one or more strands.
  • It follows all five CAS stages.

Plan it in this order

  1. Pick a need or goal you care about and can reach from where you live.
  2. Form a team of two to five with different strengths. Give each person a named role.
  3. Write a one-page proposal: aim, who benefits, timeline, resources, risks, how you will know it worked.
  4. Get the coordinator's approval before any action.
  5. Agree a meeting rhythm and keep short notes of each meeting.
  6. Run it. Take photos and keep documents as you go.
  7. Finish properly: hand over, thank partners, measure the result, present it.

Project examples

  • Service: a weekly English or maths tutoring club for younger pupils, run for a term
  • Service and creativity: a recorded audiobook library of children's stories for a local hospital or nursery
  • Creativity: a student-written and directed play, from script to performance
  • Activity and service: a charity fun run, including route, safety, sign-up and a report to the charity
  • Activity: training a group of beginners for a hiking expedition and leading it
  • Service and research: a school waste audit followed by a recycling scheme and a results report
  • Creativity and service: a bilingual guide for new families joining the school

What goes wrong

  • Starting in year 2, when coursework deadlines leave no room
  • A project that is really one person's work with names added
  • No partner contact, so the "need" was only assumed
  • A single event with no planning record and no follow-up
  • No evidence kept until the week of the final interview

Reflection

Reflection is what turns an activity into evidence. Quality matters far more than quantity.

Four things a reflection can do

  • Describe: what happened, briefly
  • Express feelings: how you reacted at the time
  • Generate ideas: what you would rethink or try next
  • Ask questions: about people, processes or issues you met

When to reflect

  • At a significant moment: a setback, a breakthrough, a surprise
  • At the start, to record your goal and what you expect
  • At the end, to compare the result with that goal
  • You do not need an entry for every session. A few thoughtful ones beat a diary of weekly summaries

Forms it can take

  • A short written entry, a voice memo or a video diary
  • An annotated photo, sketch or mind map
  • A blog post, a poem, a short film or a conversation you record notes from

Weak and strong, side by side

Weak: Today we had basketball practice. We did drills and then played a game. It was fun and I learned teamwork.

Strong: I missed four free throws in the last quarter and blamed the noise. My coach showed me that my routine changes when I am tired. For two weeks I have practised the same three-step routine after sprints, and my practice rate has gone from 5 in 10 to 7 in 10. I now see that what I called bad luck was a gap in preparation.

Weak: I helped at the food bank. It felt good to help people in need.

Strong: I expected to hand out food and was put on sorting donations instead. A third of what arrived was past its date and had to be thrown away. I had assumed any donation helps. I asked the manager what they need most, and our next school collection will use her list. It made me question who a donation is really for.

Prompts when you are stuck

  • What did I expect, and what happened instead?
  • What was the hardest moment, and what did I do?
  • What would the people I worked with say about my part?
  • What do I understand now that I did not before?
  • What will I do differently next week because of this?
  • Which learning outcome does this show, and where is the proof?

Portfolio and Interviews

The portfolio is where the school sees your evidence. The interviews are where you talk it through.

What a good portfolio holds

  • A short profile: your interests, strengths and goals for CAS
  • A list of experiences with dates, strand and supervisor
  • Evidence for each: photos, videos, plans, emails, certificates, products
  • Reflections tagged with the outcome they show
  • The project file: proposal, roles, meeting notes, result
  • A map showing which experience proves which outcome

Habits that save you later

  • Upload evidence the same week. Ten minutes on Sunday is enough
  • Name a supervisor for each experience and get their contact early
  • Ask permission before you photograph or name other people, especially children
  • Keep copies outside the school platform
  • Check the outcome map every term and plan for the gaps

The three interviews

First · start of year 1

You explain your interests and outline a plan. The adviser checks you understand the requirements. Bring a list of ideas across all three strands and a first thought about the project.

Second · around the end of year 1

A progress check: balance of strands, outcomes shown so far, the state of the project. Bring your outcome map and be honest about the gaps.

Third · near the end of the programme

A summing-up. You talk through what you achieved, show evidence for all seven outcomes and say how you have changed. Bring two or three experiences you can discuss in depth.

Exact timing is set by the school. Prepare for each as you would for an oral: know your examples, and let the adviser hear you think.

CAS problems are visible early. These are the ones worth acting on.

"You need 150 hours."False. The IB sets no hour total. It asks for 18 months of regular involvement and seven outcomes. Your school may add its own guide.
"CAS has to be impressive or overseas."False. A steady local commitment with honest reflection is stronger than an expensive trip.
"Every experience needs all seven outcomes."False. Each outcome is needed once across the whole programme.
"I already play a sport, so Activity is done."Partly. A continuing activity counts when you set a new goal or take a new role and can show growth.

"CAS can wait until after exams or coursework."False. It must run through the programme, and an incomplete CAS means no diploma whatever the score.

For Parents and Teachers

CAS belongs to the student. Adults help most by making room for it.

Parents

  • Ask what they are doing for each strand and what they enjoy, more than how many hours they have
  • Help with practical barriers: transport, timing, a contact at a local organisation
  • Let them choose. An activity picked to please an adult rarely lasts 18 months
  • Do not arrange or run the project for them. Planning it is one of the outcomes
  • Ask the school for the CAS deadlines and the dates of the three interviews
  • Once a term, ask whether the coordinator considers them on track

Teachers and advisers

  • Explain the seven outcomes with real examples in the first weeks
  • Approve experiences quickly so students can start
  • Show a strong and a weak reflection side by side
  • Review each student's outcome map every term and name the gaps
  • Set the project proposal deadline in year 1
  • Give students time in school to update portfolios
  • Check safeguarding, consent and risk for off-site work
