How this is designed
  1. Spaced repetition. Every item has its own memory model (FSRS, the open scheduler Anki also offers). It estimates how likely you are to still remember the item and brings it back when that falls to your target, 90% unless you change it. A correct answer can be re-rated Hard or Easy. Because picking from four choices is easier than recalling, a multiple-choice answer lengthens the interval less than a typed one, and new items start on shorter intervals than the FSRS default.
  2. Retrieval practice. You are always tested, never just shown. New items get a short study card, then an immediate test, then another test later in the same session.
  3. Graduated difficulty. Recognition first (pick the meaning), then recall (pick the Japanese), then production (type the reading, build the sentence, fill the blank from memory). The format gets harder as an item matures.
  4. Interleaving. Sessions mix words, kanji, sentences and grammar in random order instead of blocking one type.
  5. Comprehensible input (i+1). New sentences are unlocked only once most of their words are known, and every word is shown inside a real sentence.
  6. Kanji through words. Kanji appear when the first word that uses them appears, with every related word on the feedback card.
  7. Elaborative feedback. Each answer, right or wrong, shows the full item in context: readings, example sentences, word-by-word breakdown.
  8. Kanji in context. Every kanji in a feedback card, study card or dictionary entry is tappable: it reads the words that use it aloud and lists the sentences it appears in.
  9. Dual coding and listening. Audio on every item and dictation-style listening questions where a Japanese voice is available.
  10. Extensive reading. Short passages set in the game's prefectures, written almost entirely from the word list, with translations hidden until you ask and comprehension questions afterwards.
  11. Honest evidence. Every multiple-choice question has a Don't know button, because a lucky guess hides a gap. What you confuse an item with is remembered and offered again next time, and kanji you have met a few times are tested against their look-alikes (待 持 時).
  12. Your own hooks. When you miss an item you can write a memory hint in your own words. It is saved and shown each time that item returns.
  13. Pacing. New items arrive five at a time, pause while a backlog is waiting, and the home card estimates how long today's session will take. The placement check lets you skip what you already know. A study day ends at 4 a.m., and one missed day a week does not break a streak.
  14. From zero. A kana drill covers hiragana and katakana, including the voiced and combined sounds, tested against their look-alikes (シ ツ ソ ン). Dates and counters (ついたち, ひとり, さんがい, はたち) have their own drill, because they are irregular and come up constantly.
  15. Where the content comes from. Your original list; then words, places and grammar confirmed to be in the game from its public update notes, a community guide and reviews; then the rest of the standard beginner list. New items arrive in that order, with no more than two new kanji in one session.
  16. Nothing leaves this browser. So the page keeps a daily snapshot to undo a reset, offers a backup to copy or download, and reminds you every two weeks if you have not made one.
  17. Readings on demand. Every sentence and passage line has a kana reading and every kanji its main on and kun readings. Question prompts keep them hidden behind a かな button so you try the kanji first.
  18. Rules, not just answers. Verb forms, adjective forms, numbers, prices and clock times are generated fresh each round. The wrong options are the mistakes learners really make (帰ます, 行いて, 高いでした, さんひゃく), and the rule is stated after each answer.
  19. You have the last word. Japanese word order is flexible and typing has typos, so a built or typed answer marked wrong can be overruled, and a word you already know can skip the learning steps.
  20. Honest scheduling. Extra drilling before an item is due barely moves its interval, so cramming cannot fake long-term memory.
  21. Metacognition and habit. Flashcards use four-level self-grading; the daily goal, streak and forecast keep the load visible and sustainable.
