How this is designed
  1. Spaced repetition. Every item has its own schedule (an SM-2 style algorithm). Reviews come back just before you would forget them; easy items spread out to weeks, hard ones return in minutes.
  2. Retrieval practice. You are always tested, never just shown. New items get a short study card, then an immediate test, then another test later in the same session.
  3. Graduated difficulty. Recognition first (pick the meaning), then recall (pick the Japanese), then production (type the reading, build the sentence, fill the blank from memory). The format gets harder as an item matures.
  4. Interleaving. Sessions mix words, kanji, sentences and grammar in random order instead of blocking one type.
  5. Comprehensible input (i+1). New sentences are unlocked only once most of their words are known, and every word is shown inside a real sentence.
  6. Kanji through words. Kanji appear when the first word that uses them appears, with every related word on the feedback card.
  7. Elaborative feedback. Each answer, right or wrong, shows the full item in context: readings, example sentences, word-by-word breakdown.
  8. Kanji in context. Every kanji in a feedback card, study card or dictionary entry is tappable: it reads the words that use it aloud and lists the sentences it appears in.
  9. Dual coding and listening. Audio on every item and dictation-style listening questions where a Japanese voice is available.
  10. Extensive reading. Short passages set in the game's prefectures, written almost entirely from the word list, with translations hidden until you ask and comprehension questions afterwards.
  11. Metacognition and habit. Flashcards use four-level self-grading; the daily goal, streak and forecast keep the load visible and sustainable.
